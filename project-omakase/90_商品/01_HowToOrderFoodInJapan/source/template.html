Part 1

Before You Go

行く前に知っておくこと

Six things every Japanese person knows — and almost no first-time visitor does.

1. No tipping. Ever.

Service is part of the price. If you leave money on the table, a staff member may run after you to give it back. A sincere "thank you" is the tip.

2. Water and tea are free

They often arrive as soon as you sit down. At some shops it's self-service — look for a water jug or dispenser near the counter.

3. "Sumimasen!" calls staff

Raise your hand and call out sumimasen ("excuse me"). It isn't rude — it's how it works. Many tables also have a call button.

4. The wet towel is for your hands

That's the oshibori. Use it to clean your hands before eating. Leave it folded beside your plate.

5. Cash still matters

Cards and IC cards (Suica, PASMO, ICOCA) are accepted in more places every year, but small, long-running shops can be cash-only. Carry ¥1,000 notes and coins.

6. Most places are non-smoking

Since April 2020, restaurants are non-smoking indoors by default. Some small, long-established bars and izakaya may still allow it — look for a sign at the entrance.

Ticket Machines

券売機・食券機

At many ramen, soba, udon and set-meal shops you pay before you sit down. If there's a machine near the door, that's where your meal starts.

  1. Insert money firstNotes or coins. Lit buttons show what you can now buy. Some newer machines let you choose first, then pay by card or IC card.
  2. Press your dishOne button = one ticket.
  3. Add extrasLarge size, egg, rice — each is a separate button and a separate ticket.
  4. Take your ticket(s) and changeIf change doesn't drop, press おつり (otsuri).
  5. Hand the tickets to staff…or place them on the counter in front of your seat. Then sit where you're shown.

Bring ¥1,000 notes

Some machines don't accept ¥10,000 notes, or the new banknotes issued in 2024. Keep a few ¥1,000 notes and coins for ramen shops.

Can't read the buttons?

Buttons with photos are your friend. The top-left button is often the shop's signature dish. Point your phone's camera translator at the machine — or show card 12 to staff.

Tablet & QR Ordering

タブレット・QRコード注文

More and more restaurants let you order from a tablet at your table or from your own phone. Usually it's the easiest way to order — once you know the words.

TABLET AT YOUR TABLE
  1. Find the language buttonLook for EN, English, 言語 or a globe icon.
  2. Add items to your orderChoose quantity, then add.
  3. Confirm the orderPress 注文する or 注文確定. Nothing arriving? You probably didn't confirm.
QR CODE ON THE TABLE
  1. Scan the codewith your phone camera. The menu opens in your browser.
  2. Order from your phoneYour order history stays on the page.
  3. Check how to paySome shops take payment on the phone; most still ask you to pay at the register.

Izakaya

居酒屋

A Japanese pub where the food matters as much as the drinks. Dishes are small, shared, and ordered a few at a time — the evening unfolds in rounds.

The dish you didn't order: お通し (otōshi)

Soon after you sit down, a small dish arrives that you didn't ask for. That's the otoshi — think of it as a seat charge that comes with a snack.

It's usually about ¥300–500 per person, but can be ¥1,000 or more at upscale places or in tourist areas. On the bill it may appear as "charge" or "seat fee" (チャージ／席料). It's normally not something you can refuse.

If it matters to you, ask before you sit: お通しはありますか？ (Otōshi wa arimasu ka?) — "Is there an otoshi charge?"

How an evening works

  1. Drinks come firstStaff will ask for your first drink almost immediately. "Toriaezu biiru" — "beer for now" — is the classic opener. You don't have to order food yet.
  2. Order a few dishes, not everythingTwo or three to start. More will follow.
  3. Everything is sharedDishes go in the middle of the table. Ask for small plates: 取り皿 (torizara).
  4. Order more in roundsCall "sumimasen!" whenever you want the next round.
  5. "Last order"Staff announce ラストオーダー before closing — your final chance to add anything.

飲み放題 (nomihōdai) — all you can drink

A fixed price for unlimited drinks within a time limit, often 90–120 minutes. Many places ask everyone at the table to take it if one person does. Ask the time limit and the last-order time.

〆 (shime) — the finisher

Many people end the night with something simple — rice, noodles or ochazuke (tea poured over rice). If you see 〆 on the menu, that's the section.

Reading a menu with no English? Many izakaya have a handwritten board of today's specials. Photograph it and run it through your phone's translator — or simply point at what the next table is eating and show card 6.

Conveyor-Belt Sushi

回転寿司

Cheap, fun and fast — and the big chains are built for people who don't speak Japanese. Here's what to expect from the door to the bill.

  1. Check in at the entranceMany chains have a reception machine. Choose table or counter and the number of people; take the ticket and wait until your number appears on the screen.
  2. Switch the touch panel to EnglishEach seat has a screen. Most big chains offer English and other languages.
  3. Order on the panelWhat you order arrives on a separate lane or a marked plate, and the screen tells you when it's yours. Take only your own order.
  4. Or take plates from the beltAt shops where sushi still circulates, you can take any plate that passes.
  5. Ask for the bill on the panelPress the check button (お会計), then pay at the register on the way out.

Never put a plate back

Once a plate is off the belt, it's yours. Stack your empty plates — some chains have a slot at your seat to drop them into, which counts them for you.

How much is a plate?

Some chains price by plate colour (there's a chart on the wall or panel); others are mostly flat-price. Basic plates start around ¥110 at the big chains.

Paying & Leaving

お会計

Paying is simple once you know one rule: look for the bill on your table.

Bill on the table → pay at the register

If staff left a slip on your table (often in a holder), take it to the register by the exit when you're done.

No bill → ask at your table

Say "Okaikei onegaishimasu" (card 14). Staff will bring the bill or point you to the register.

Use the small tray

At the register there's usually a small tray. Place your cash or card on it rather than handing it over directly. Your change comes back on the tray, too.

Splitting the bill

Ask "Betsubetsu de onegaishimasu" (card 16). Many casual places will split it; when it's busy, some prefer a single payment.

The words that make staff smile

On your way out, say ごちそうさまでした (gochisōsama deshita) — "thank you for the meal." It's what Japanese customers say, and staff genuinely appreciate hearing it from visitors.

Reminder: no tip — not on the table, not in the tray. The price you see is the price you pay.

Part 7

Etiquette That Actually Matters

本当に大事なマナーだけ

Visitors often worry far too much about "doing it wrong." Here's the short list that matters — and the longer list you can stop worrying about.

Worth getting right

Chopsticks: two things to avoid

Don't pass food from your chopsticks to someone else's, and don't stand chopsticks upright in rice. Both are associated with funerals.

Wait to be seated

Don't take an empty table on your own unless staff gesture for you to sit anywhere.

Eating while walking

In some busy tourist areas it's discouraged. If you buy street food, eat it by the stall where you bought it.

You can relax about

Slurping noodles

Perfectly normal. Nobody will think twice.

Lifting your bowl

Holding a rice or soup bowl up near your mouth is the normal way to eat.

Not speaking Japanese

Nobody expects you to. A smile, pointing and the cards in this guide are more than enough.
